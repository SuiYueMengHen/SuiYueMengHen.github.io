# Electric Vehicles

Is Driving an Electric Vehicle Good?

An [electric vehicle](https://www.britannica.com/technology/electric-car) (EV) is a car, truck, boat, plane, or other mechanized means of carrying people that is at least partially powered by an electric motor drawing energy from a rechargeable battery.

EVs fall into four major categories:

- **BEV**: Battery EVs, called "fully electric" or "all-electric" and sometimes simply dubbed "EVs," are powered entirely by electricity from a rechargeable battery and have no gasoline engine. They are charged ("refueled") by plugging them into an external power source, such as a standard 120-[volt](https://www.britannica.com/science/volt-unit-of-measurement) home outlet (called Level 1 charging, the slowest method), a faster 208- to 240-volt home outlet generally used for larger appliances (called Level 2 charging), or a direct-current fast-charging (DCFC) station (the fastest method, called Level 3 charging). While the vehicle is driven, the battery is also recharged through [regenerative braking](https://auto.howstuffworks.com/auto-parts/brakes/brake-types/regenerative-braking.htm), which converts the vehicle's [kinetic energy](https://www.britannica.com/science/kinetic-energy) into electricity as it slows down.

- **PHEV**: Plug-in hybrid EVs have both an electric motor with a large battery and a [gasoline engine](https://www.britannica.com/technology/gasoline-engine) with a fuel tank, giving them the flexibility to use electricity, gasoline, or both. Their large battery is charged through any of the methods listed above for BEVs, allowing for a significant all-electric driving range before the gasoline engine is needed.

- **HEV**: Hybrid EVs (often simply called "hybrids") use traditional gasoline-powered combustion engines with a supportive electric motor and battery (one smaller than those in PHEVs). Its battery, used mainly to assist the gas engine, is charged by the gas engine and through regenerative braking. HEVs do not have a charging port and thus cannot be plugged in to charge.

- **FCEV**: [Fuel cell](https://www.britannica.com/technology/fuel-cell) EVs are powered by hydrogen gas stored in a tank (similar to a gas tank for a [combustion engine](https://www.britannica.com/technology/internal-combustion-engine)) that is converted to electricity by a fuel cell; they are not "charged" and plugged in like BEVs but are refueled with hydrogen at a dedicated station, similar to how a gas-powered car is refueled.

Electric vehicles may seem new because of their recent popularity, but the first EV dates to the early 19th century. [Hungarian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Hungary) [Benedictine](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Benedictines) monk Ányos Jedlik invented one of the first electric motors in 1827 or 1828 and used it to power a small model car that is now in the permanent collection of the [Hungarian Museum of Science, Technology and Transport](https://pestbuda.hu/en/cikk/20230102_the_museum_of_technology_was_founded_three_times_unparalleled_hungarian_memories_are_preserved_in_the_collection_created_50_years_ago).

Many subsequent inventions laid the groundwork for the first successful commercially available electric car: the Electrobat. Invented in 1894 by mechanical engineer Henry G. Morris and chemist Pedro G. Salom in [Philadelphia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Philadelphia), the car weighed about 4,400 pounds, with the rechargeable batteries accounting for almost 1,600 pounds of that weight. For comparison, a 2025 four-door [Honda](https://www.britannica.com/money/Honda-Motor-Company-Ltd) Civic base model weighs 2,877 pounds.

Electrobats were quiet vehicles commonly used as taxis in major cities in the late 1890s, but their popularity was fleeting. The debut of the [Ford Model T](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Model-T) in 1908 all but killed the market for electric cars. [Henry Ford's](https://www.britannica.com/money/Henry-Ford) combustion engine car was twice as fast, had twice the range, and was much cheaper than the top-performing EV of the time, the Detroit Electric. By 1921 the base model Ford car cost just $370—about 12 percent of the Detroit Electric's $2,985 starting price tag. Furthermore, as gasoline became more widely available after the discovery of [crude oil](https://www.britannica.com/science/crude-oil) in [Texas](https://www.britannica.com/place/Texas-state) (in Corsicana in 1894 and at Spindletop Hill in 1901), gas-powered cars rose in popularity.

Interest in EVs was renewed by the environmental movement of the 1960s (which brought increased state and federal regulations on gas-car emissions) and the [oil crises](https://www.britannica.com/money/oil-crisis) and gas shortages of the 1970s (which caused gas prices to skyrocket). The U.S. Congress passed the [Electric and Hybrid Vehicle Research, Development, and Demonstration Act in 1976](https://www.congress.gov/bill/94th-congress/house-bill/8800) (after overriding Pres. [Gerald Ford](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gerald-Ford)'s veto of the bill) to [General Motors](https://www.britannica.com/money/General-Motors-Corporation) (GM) produced an electric car based on the Chevrolet Corvair and an electric van, aptly named the Electrovair and Electrovan, respectively. The EVs of the 1960s and '70s were generally small, short-range vehicles that were best used for city driving.

[NASA](https://www.britannica.com/topic/NASA) even took EVs to the Moon. GM and [Boeing](https://www.britannica.com/money/Boeing-Company) made electric Lunar Roving Vehicles, which astronauts drove across the [Moon's](https://www.britannica.com/place/Moon) surface on the last three Apollo missions (Apollo 15 in 1971 and Apollo 16 and 17 in 1972).

Interest in EVs continued into the late 20th century as concern about [climate change](https://www.britannica.com/science/climate-change) once again made the vehicles popular. In 1990 [California](https://www.britannica.com/place/California-state) passed strict laws that required the [auto industry](https://www.britannica.com/technology/automotive-industry) to manufacture zero-emissions vehicles. As a result, GM manufactured 1,117 of the EV1, the first American mass-produced EV, between 1996 and 1999. But the cars were only available for three-year leases (not for purchase), and, after California loosened the emissions law, GM reclaimed the cars and crushed most of them because they were expensive to manufacture and replacement parts were scarce. About 40 of them survive, including one in Los Angeles's [Petersen Automotive Museum](https://www.facebook.com/PetersenMuseum/posts/the-ev1-was-the-first-modern-mass-produced-electric-vehicle-ev-built-by-a-major-/10160209516127402/) and another in the [Henry Ford Museum of American Innovation](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections-and-research/digital-collections/artifact/326534/) in [Dearborn](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dearborn), Michigan.

Perhaps the most recognizable contemporary EV is the hybrid [Toyota](https://www.britannica.com/money/Toyota-Motor-Corporation) Prius, which was launched in October 1997 in Japan. The 2001 models arrived in [North America](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-America) in 2000, and by September 2025, Toyota had sold some 6–7 million of them on the continent.

While most car manufacturers now produce hybrids or EVs in addition to gas-powered cars, few manufacturers only produce EVs. Of those—which include [BYD](https://www.britannica.com/money/BYD-Co-Ltd), Lucid Motors, Polestar, Rivian, and XPeng—[Tesla](https://www.britannica.com/money/Tesla-Motors) has become the most globally recognizable EV brand. Founded in 2003 by American entrepreneurs [Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning](https://www.britannica.com/money/Martin-Eberhard-and-Marc-Tarpenning), funded in part by [Elon Musk](https://www.britannica.com/money/Elon-Musk), and named after Serbian American inventor [Nikola Tesla](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nikola-Tesla), the company released its first EV, the Roadster, in 2008.

In 2024 Tesla sold the most EVs in the United States with 633,762 units sold, followed by GM (including Chevrolet, Cadillac, and GMC EVs) with 112,897 units. Though sales of all EVs dropped slightly during the first six months of 2025, there was a surge of sales in the third quarter (July through September), likely due to expiring federal tax credits that would not be available after September 30. However, even with the record increase in sales, EVs still made up only 10.5 percent of all vehicle sales in the U.S.

Globally, EVs were 22 percent of all new cars sold in 2024. [Nordic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nordic-countries) countries had the largest share of new-car sales of EVs: [Norway](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norway) at 92 percent (113,500 cars), followed by [Sweden](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sweden) at 58 percent (157,000 cars), Denmark at 56 percent (96,100 cars), and Finland at 50 percent (37,000 cars). China, however, had the most new EVs on the road (11.3 million cars). Still, EVs account for only 4.5 percent of all cars on the road globally.

## Pro Arguments

### Pro 1: EVs are better for the environment than gas-powered cars.

EVs not only emit no harmful emissions while in operation, but they can even be powered with electricity from alternative energy sources (such as solar panels). Furthermore, the batteries can be recycled.

As NPR journalist Camila Domonoske explains, building an electric vehicle does more damage to the climate than building a gas car does. But the gas car starts to catch up as soon as it goes its first mile…[because] building a battery is an environmental cost that's paid once. Burning gasoline is a cost that's paid again, and again, and again.

Moreover, while EV batteries are currently fossil-fuel intensive thanks to the use of fossil-fuel energy needed to make them, two cradle-to-grave lifecycle analysis studies show that EVs have a lower climate impact overall and are thus better for the environment than gas-powered cars.

When it comes to protecting the environment, we can't always be perfect, but we can be better. Electric cars help us to be better stewards of the environment.

### Pro 2: EVs are less expensive to fuel and maintain.

EVs have fewer moving parts than gas-powered cars, which translates to less maintenance. Moreover, electricity is cheaper than gas when it comes to powering cars. A Wall Street Journal survey found that EV drivers in 15 major American cities saved between $428 and $899 per year over gas-powered car drivers. Over the course of 100,000 miles, EV drivers in those 15 cities could save between $4,000 and $9,000 in fuel costs alone.

Plugless Power, an EV charger manufacturer, estimates that even in Connecticut, which is among the states with the highest residential energy costs, EV drivers still save about $29 monthly in fuel costs.

As Chris Hardesty of Cox Automotive notes: EV drivers don't need to worry about engine oil or transmission fluid in their cars. There aren't many fluids to monitor in an EV…[and] regenerative braking reduces wear and tear on an electric car's brake pads, making their replacement less frequent than for gas cars.

### Pro 3: EVs are quiet and offer better handling than gas-powered cars.

As environmentalist David M. Kuchta explains, EVs are naturally quieter than gasoline-powered cars because they lack internal combustion engines. On traditional cars, the engine makes a combustion noise as cylinder pressure changes. The only noise made by EVs comes from their tires and the wind resistance while driving.

While the lack of noise has led to concerns about pedestrian and animal safety (their quiet nature makes them difficult to hear), the United States, Australia, and the European Union have all required new EVs to make sounds when they are traveling at low speeds (at higher speeds they naturally emit more noise).

EV drivers also find the cars nice to drive. Because EVs have a lower center of gravity, they have better handling and responsiveness. Electric motors also accelerate and decelerate more smoothly.

As the American Lung Association notes, Another benefit of EVs over gas powered vehicles is improved acceleration and more horsepower. Electric car motors don't have a transmission and deliver the power directly to the wheels. As a result of this, they can go from 0–60 much faster than a traditional car.…Electric motors can also use their horsepower more efficiently because they have fewer moving parts.

## Con Arguments

### Con 1: EVs are not carbon-neutral, they still damage the environment, and their batteries can be dangerous.

Most EVs rely on lithium-ion batteries that are made with lithium and cobalt. The environmental impact of lithium mining is particularly high.

Martinia Igini, editor in chief of Earth.Org, explains, Lithium mining is a process known for being extremely detrimental to the environment as well as incredibly resource-intensive, with side effects including water loss and contamination, ground destabilization, and biodiversity loss.

In fact, processing lithium produces "nearly 15 tonnes [of CO2 emissions] for every tonne of mined lithium."

Moreover, post-production EVs still create pollution because, if the cars are not charged with green energy, they still rely on electricity from fossil fuels, mainly coal to charge their batteries.

Finally, EVs use lithium-ion batteries that can explode randomly while driving, in a wreck, and even while charging. While combustion engines may be more likely to explode, EV battery fires are more difficult to extinguish, can reignite, and may emit fumes containing a highly toxic cocktail of hazardous gases, including hydrogen fluoride, carbon monoxide, and hydrogen cyanide.

### Con 2: EVs cost more to buy, and thus finance, than gas-powered cars.

In the U.S. in 2025, the least expensive EV, the Nissan Leaf, started at $29,280. In comparison, the 2025 (gas-powered) Nissan Versa, the least expensive car in the U.S., started at $17,190, or about 41 percent less than the Leaf.

Furthermore, the federal tax credits for new and used EVs (up to $7,500 and $4,000, respectively), established under the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, were repealed on September 30, 2025.

The upfront costs of an EV are also prohibitive for many car buyers. EV owners may even have to pay registration and title fees levied specifically on EVs by states to compensate for the minimal or nonexistent taxes they are paying on gas. As U.S. News reports, Oregon, for example, has one of the best EV rebate programs in the U.S. but charges buyers of electric cars more than double the rate for registration than non-EV buyers and nearly double the title price. In neighboring Idaho, there is no tax credit or rebate program, and the state charges EV drivers an annual fee of $140. PHEV [plug-in hybrid electric vehicle] drivers in Idaho have to pay $75 per year, in addition to the state's standard registration fees.

In all, a majority of states in 2025 had such fees for EV buyers, and many offered no tax credits to offset the higher costs and additional fees of an EV.

According to one estimate, fewer than 15 percent of Americans can afford to buy an EV.

### Con 3: EVs are less reliable than gas-powered cars and lack the infrastructure needed to support them.

In a 2024 Pew Research survey of American adults, 50 percent said EVs were less reliable than gas-powered cars, which is an increase of 16 percent from 2021.

There's good reason for this opinion. Consumer Reports found that EVs have 42 percent more problems and are less reliable in general than both gas-powered and hybrid cars. Moreover, PHEVs (plug-in hybrid vehicles that combine an electric motor and battery with a combustion engine) have 70 percent more problems.

In 2024 there was an average of 104 gas pumps per 1,000 road miles. On the other hand, there was an average of only 22 EV charging stations per 1,000 road miles. Plus, these charging stations are not distributed evenly; they are heavily concentrated in urban centers.

Consumer sentiment reflects this "range anxiety." A 2025 AAA survey found that 56 percent of those polled cited "a lack of convenient public charging stations," and 55 percent cited a "fear of running out of charge while driving" as reasons for not buying a fully electric car.

After trying to drive his electric F-150 Lightning truck on a long road trip, even the CEO of Ford Motor Company had to admit that "charging has been pretty challenging." His story mirrors that of a Canadian man who encountered so many charging problems while driving from Winnipeg to Chicago in his Lightning that he had to rent a gas-powered vehicle to complete his journey.

## Pro and Con: Alternative Energy

To access extended pro and con arguments, sources, and discussion questions about whether alternative energy can effectively replace fossil fuels, go to [ProCon.org](https://alternativeenergy.procon.org/).

Whether alternative energy can meet energy demands effectively enough to phase out finite [fossil fuels](https://www.britannica.com/science/fossil-fuel) (such as [coal](https://www.britannica.com/science/coal-fossil-fuel), [oil](https://www.britannica.com/science/oil-chemical-compound), and [natural gas](https://www.britannica.com/science/natural-gas)) is hotly debated. Alternative energies include [renewable sources](https://www.britannica.com/science/renewable-energy)—such as [solar](https://www.britannica.com/science/solar-energy), [tidal](https://www.britannica.com/science/tidal-power), [wind](https://www.britannica.com/science/wind-energy), [biofuel](https://www.britannica.com/technology/biofuel), [hydroelectric](https://www.britannica.com/science/hydroelectric-power), and [geothermal](https://www.britannica.com/science/geothermal-energy)—and nonrenewable [nuclear power](https://www.britannica.com/technology/nuclear-power).

Globally, fossil fuels have been used for energy for much of human history. The [Chinese](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-China) were the first to transition to fossil fuels from wood fire energy. They used coal as early as 2000 bce, [natural gas](https://www.britannica.com/science/natural-gas) since 200 bce, and [petroleum](https://www.britannica.com/science/petroleum) since the 1st century ce. Europeans developed hydropower in 200 bce, and Persians developed [windmills](https://www.britannica.com/technology/windmill) in the 10th century. The famed [Dutch](https://www.britannica.com/place/Netherlands) windmills wouldn't be built until the 1590s. Other energies, both fossil and alternative, are relatively new for energy uses, appearing in the 19th and 20th centuries. See [ProCon's "Historical Timeline: History of Alternative Energy and Fossil Fuels."](https://alternativeenergy.procon.org/historical-timeline/)

By 2022, energy consumption in the United States remained primarily fossil fuels: 9.89 percent coal, 33.35 percent natural gas, and 35.32 percent petroleum (78.50 percent total). Renewable energy sources accounted for 8.09 percent of energy consumption: 0.87 percent hydroelectric, 0.12 percent geothermal, 0.76 percent solar, 1.48 percent wind, and 4.86 percent biomass. Nuclear energy (considered alternative but not renewable) accounted for 8.06 percent of U.S. energy use. The totals do not equal 100 percent due to decimal rounding.

## [Pro](https://alternativeenergy.procon.org/)

- Alternative energies not only can but must replace fossil fuels if we want to continue living on Earth.
- Many countries are already operating on significant renewable energy sources.
- Nuclear energy, a low-carbon source of alternative energy, is the quickest way to end dependence on fossil fuels.

## [Con](https://alternativeenergy.procon.org/)

- Natural gas, a fossil fuel, is an appropriate and necessary bridge fuel to meet net-zero emissions goals.
- The idea that renewable energies will fill the energy needs of large countries any time soon is counterproductive and hinders realistic change for the better.
- Nuclear energy is too dangerous and ineffective to be a serious antidote to global warming.

# 电动汽车

**驾驶电动汽车是好事吗？**

电动汽车（EV）是指由可充电电池提供能量、由电动机至少部分驱动的汽车、卡车、船、飞机或其他机械载具。

电动汽车分为四大类：

- **BEV（纯电池电动汽车）**：也称“全电动车”或直接简称“EV”，完全由可充电电池中的电力驱动，没有汽油发动机。通过插入外部电源“补能”，例如标准的120伏家用插座（称为一级充电，最慢）、用于大型电器的208–240伏家用插座（二级充电）或直流快速充电站（最快，三级充电）。行驶中，车辆减速时还会通过**再生制动**将动能转化为电能，为电池充电。

- **PHEV（插电式混合动力电动汽车）**：同时拥有带大容量电池的电动机和带油箱的汽油发动机，可灵活使用电力、汽油或两者兼用。大电池可通过上述任一种方式充电，因此纯电续航里程较长，之后才需要汽油发动机介入。

- **HEV（混合动力电动汽车）**：通常简称“混合动力”，使用传统汽油内燃机，辅以电动机和较小电池（比PHEV小）。电池主要用于辅助汽油机，由汽油机和再生制动充电。HEV没有充电口，因此不能外接充电。

- **FCEV（燃料电池电动汽车）**：由储存在油箱（类似内燃机的油箱）中的氢气驱动，氢气在燃料电池中转化为电能；它们不像BEV那样“充电”和插电，而是在专用加氢站加氢，类似于加油。

电动汽车看似因近年流行而显得新鲜，但第一辆电动汽车可追溯至19世纪初。匈牙利本笃会修士阿纽什·耶德利克于1827或1828年发明了最早的电动机之一，并用它驱动了一辆小型模型车，该车现藏于匈牙利科技交通博物馆。

许多后续发明为第一辆成功商用的电动汽车——Electrobat 奠定了基础。1894年，机械工程师亨利·G·莫里斯和化学家佩德罗·G·萨洛姆在费城发明了这款车，重约4400磅，其中可充电电池就占了近1600磅。作为对比，2025款四门本田思域基础款重2877磅。

Electrobat 是安静的车辆，在1890年代末常被用作大城市的出租车，但它的流行转瞬即逝。1908年福特T型车问世，几乎扼杀了电动汽车市场。亨利·福特的燃油车速度是当时顶尖电动车（底特律电气）的两倍，续航也是两倍，价格却便宜得多。到1921年，福特基础款仅售370美元，约为底特律电气起售价2985美元的12%。此外，得克萨斯州（1894年科西卡纳和1901年斯平德尔托普山）发现原油后，汽油更易获得，燃油车越来越受欢迎。

1960年代环保运动（促使各州和联邦加强对汽车排放的监管）和1970年代石油危机及汽油短缺（导致油价飙升）再次激发了人们对电动汽车的兴趣。美国国会1976年通过了《电动和混合动力汽车研究、开发与示范法案》（推翻杰拉尔德·福特总统的否决），通用汽车据此制造了基于雪佛兰Corvair的电动汽车和电动货车，分别命名为 Electrovair 和 Electrovan。1960-70年代的电动车通常体型小、续航短，适合城市驾驶。

美国宇航局甚至把电动车带上了月球。通用和波音制造了电动月球车，宇航员在最后三次阿波罗任务（1971年阿波罗15号，1972年阿波罗16号和17号）中驾驶它们穿越月球表面。

20世纪末，对气候变化的关注再次使电动汽车流行起来。1990年加利福尼亚州通过严格法律，要求汽车行业生产零排放车辆。因此，通用汽车在1996至1999年间制造了1117辆 EV1，这是美国首款大规模生产的电动车。但这些车仅提供三年租赁（不可购买），后来加州放宽排放法，通用因制造成本高、配件稀缺而收回并销毁了大部分。现存约40辆，包括洛杉矶彼得森汽车博物馆和密歇根州迪尔伯恩亨利·福特博物馆各一辆。

也许最知名的当代电动车是丰田普锐斯混合动力车，1997年10月在日本上市，2001款于2000年进入北美，到2025年9月，丰田在北美已售出约600-700万辆。

如今大多数汽车制造商在燃油车之外都生产混合动力或电动车，但只生产电动车的厂商较少——包括比亚迪、Lucid Motors、Polestar、Rivian和小鹏等——其中特斯拉已成为全球最知名的电动车品牌。特斯拉由美国企业家马丁·艾伯哈德和马克·塔彭宁于2003年创立，部分资金来自埃隆·马斯克，以塞尔维亚裔美国发明家尼古拉·特斯拉命名，2008年推出首款电动车Roadster。

2024年，特斯拉在美国电动车销量最高，达633762辆，其次是通用（包括雪佛兰、凯迪拉克和GMC电动车）112897辆。尽管2025上半年电动车销量略有下降，但第三季度（7-9月）销量激增，很可能因为联邦税收抵免于9月30日到期。然而，即使销量创纪录增长，电动车在美国汽车总销量中仅占10.5%。

全球范围内，2024年电动车占新车销量的22%。北欧国家电动车新车销量占比最高：挪威92%（113500辆），瑞典58%（157000辆），丹麦56%（96100辆），芬兰50%（37000辆）。但中国道路上新增电动车最多（1130万辆）。不过，电动车在全球汽车总数中仅占4.5%。

## 正方论点

### 论点1：电动车比燃油车更环保

电动车不仅在运行中不排放有害尾气，而且可以使用替代能源（如太阳能）产生的电力。此外，电池可回收利用。

正如NPR记者卡米拉·多莫诺斯克解释的：制造电动车对气候的破坏比制造燃油车更大，但燃油车从行驶第一英里起就开始追平差距……因为制造电池是一次性环境成本，而燃烧汽油是反复付出的成本。

此外，虽然当前电池制造依赖化石燃料能源，但两项“从摇篮到坟墓”的生命周期分析研究表明，电动车整体气候影响较低，因此比燃油车更环保。

在保护环境方面，我们无法永远完美，但可以做得更好。电动车帮助我们成为更好的环境管理者。

### 论点2：电动车燃料和维护成本更低

电动车活动部件比燃油车少，因此维护更少。而且电力比汽油便宜。《华尔街日报》调查显示，美国15个大城市的电动车司机每年比燃油车司机节省428至899美元。行驶10万英里，可节省4000至9000美元燃料费。

电动车充电器制造商Plugless Power估计，即使在住宅能源成本最高的康涅狄格州，电动车司机每月仍可节省约29美元燃料费。

Cox Automotive的克里斯·哈迪斯蒂指出：电动车司机无需担心机油或变速箱油，需要监测的液体很少……再生制动减少了刹车片磨损，更换频率低于燃油车。

### 论点3：电动车安静且操控性更好

环保人士大卫·M·库奇塔解释：电动车天然比燃油车安静，因为没有内燃机。传统汽车的发动机在气缸压力变化时会产生燃烧噪音。电动车唯一的噪音来自轮胎和行驶风阻。

虽然安静引发了行人和动物安全问题（难以被听到），但美国、澳大利亚和欧盟已要求新电动车在低速时发出声音（高速时自然产生更多噪音）。

电动车司机也觉得驾驶感良好。由于重心更低，操控性和响应性更好。电动机加减速更平顺。

美国肺脏协会指出：电动车相对燃油车的另一个优点是加速更快、马力更大。电动机没有变速箱，动力直接传到车轮，因此0-60英里/小时加速比传统车快得多……电动机因活动部件少，能更高效地利用马力。

## 反方论点

### 论点1：电动车并非碳中和，仍破坏环境，且电池可能危险

多数电动车使用锂离子电池，含锂和钴。锂矿开采的环境影响尤其严重。

Earth.Org主编玛蒂娜·伊吉尼解释：锂矿开采过程对环境极其有害且资源密集，副作用包括水资源流失和污染、地面不稳定及生物多样性丧失。

实际上，开采锂会“每开采1吨锂产生近15吨二氧化碳排放”。

此外，生产后的电动车仍会造成污染，因为如果不用绿色能源充电，它们依然依赖化石燃料（主要是煤）发电。

最后，电动车锂离子电池可能在行驶、碰撞甚至充电时意外爆炸。虽然燃油车可能更容易起火，但电动车电池火情更难扑灭，可能复燃，并释放含氟化氢、一氧化碳和氰化氢等剧毒混合气体的烟雾。

### 论点2：电动车购车及融资成本高于燃油车

2025年美国最便宜的电动车日产Leaf起价29280美元，而最便宜的燃油车日产Versa起价17190美元，比Leaf便宜约41%。

此外，2022年《通胀削减法案》规定的新旧电动车联邦税收抵免（最高7500美元和4000美元）已于2025年9月30日废除。

电动车的前期成本对许多购车者来说也过高。电动车车主甚至可能需支付各州专门针对电动车征收的注册和所有权费，以弥补其很少或不缴纳的燃油税。据《美国新闻》报道，俄勒冈州虽提供全美最佳电动车退税之一，但对电动车买家收取的注册费是非电动车买家的两倍多，所有权费也近乎两倍。邻近的爱达荷州没有税收抵免或退税，电动车司机每年需支付140美元年费，插电式混合动力车主则需付75美元，另加标准注册费。

总体而言，2025年大多数州都有此类收费，且许多州不提供税收抵免来抵消电动车更高的成本和额外费用。

据估计，只有不到15%的美国人能买得起电动车。

### 论点3：电动车可靠性不如燃油车，且缺乏配套基础设施

2024年皮尤研究中心对成人的调查显示，50%的美国人认为电动车可靠性低于燃油车，比2021年上升了16%。

这种观点有充分理由。《消费者报告》发现，电动车平均比燃油车和混合动力车问题多42%，可靠性更差；插电式混合动力车的问题则多出70%。

2024年，每1000英里道路平均有104个加油站，而电动车充电站仅约22个。而且这些充电站分布不均，高度集中在城市中心。

消费者情绪反映了这种“里程焦虑”。2025年AAA调查显示，56%的受访者因“缺乏便利的公共充电站”，55%因“害怕行驶中电量耗尽”而不购买全电动车。

福特汽车公司CEO在尝试驾驶电动F-150 Lightning长途旅行后也不得不承认“充电相当麻烦”。他的经历与一位加拿大男子类似，该男子驾驶Lightning从温尼伯到芝加哥途中遇到诸多充电问题，最后不得不租用燃油车完成旅程。
